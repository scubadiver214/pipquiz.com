# PipQuiz

A brief for an elementary school leader. November 2026. The product is in design. This note is an invitation to react, not a request to adopt.

## Mission

PipQuiz helps children ages 4 to 10 practice attention, memory, and the habits of learning in short sessions that feel like play. A parent holds the account. The child meets one clear task at a time, can pause, and keeps a private record of effort. We want practice to be encouraging enough that a child comes back tomorrow.

PipQuiz is an educational activity. It is not a medical treatment, and it does not diagnose ADHD or any other condition.

## The idea

Many children can do the work of school when the task is short, the instruction is plain, and nobody is timing them into a mistake. At home, the usual screen does the opposite: it is endless, loud, and built to hold attention rather than to train it.

PipQuiz is a different kind of screen time. A session is about ten minutes. The child might search a picture for one change, repeat a short pattern, solve a small math question, wait on purpose when the instruction says wait, or choose a kind thing to say at a dinner table. Then it stops. Stars record the best effort. They are not taken away for a bad day.

The hope is modest and specific. A child who practices noticing, remembering, holding back, and trying again may get better at those acts inside the activity, and may be more willing to start the next hard thing. We will not claim that this transfers to the classroom until that has actually been studied.

## Who it is for

The first audience is families with children from about age 4 through 10, which covers pre-kindergarten through the upper elementary years. Two bands keep the same games and change the load: younger children see fewer choices and shorter instructions; older children read more and hold longer sequences.

The design is especially suited to children who drift, frustrate quickly, or do better when a task has a visible end. That includes many children with attention difficulties. It also includes children who simply need a calmer way to practice. Families do not enter a diagnosis to use it.

## What children practice

Each activity is original, short, and one skill at a time.

- **Attention.** Find the one item that changed, or the one that matches.
- **Memory.** Watch a short sequence and repeat it.
- **Self-control.** Tap when told to tap, and wait when told to wait. Speed is never the grade.
- **Logic and early mathematics.** Complete a pattern, walk a small maze, count, add, and subtract.
- **Looking carefully.** Match a shape to its shadow, or follow a thick path on the screen with a finger.
- **Courtesy.** Choose a respectful response at a shared table. Please, thank you, waiting, and listening. More than one polite answer can be right. The activities do not teach that one region's manners are required of every family.
- **A story.** One short branching tale in which a choice and a counting question sit inside the same scene.

A child who stays with it unlocks a simple studio for building an avatar from preset parts. That is a prize for persistence. There is no public ranking, no chat, and no purchase inside the child's screens.

## What the adult sees

The parent creates the account, adds each child, and chooses who is playing. Progress stays in the household. Two children in one family can see their own stars. Nobody else can. The parent can turn on a calmer presentation: less motion, no confetti, sound off.

There is no teacher login in the version we are building first, and no path for a school to hold student records inside PipQuiz. If a school and a family ever used it together, the family would remain the owner of the account.

## What we are asking of you

We are early. Nothing is in front of children yet. Before we build further, we want the judgment of someone who watches attention, frustration, and family partnership every day.

Three questions would tell us whether this is worth pursuing with schools in mind:

1. Does a ten-minute, parent-guided practice of this kind meet a need you actually hear from families?
2. What would you need to see, and what would you need us never to claim, before you would mention it to a parent?
3. If the home version proved sound, what collaboration would be appropriate later, and what would be a mistake? A teacher looking at sample activities is a different thing from a school account full of children.

We would value a conversation, a critique of the activities, or a refusal. A clear no is useful. So is a narrower yes.

## What success would mean

A child finishes a short session, wants to do another tomorrow, and a parent can see what was practiced without turning the child into a score on a public board. Over time, if families stay and if educators recognize the activities as honest practice, PipQuiz can become a small membership product that pays for itself.

The goal is not to replace teaching, intervention, or a clinician. The goal is a better ten minutes.
